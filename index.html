The largest increase in corporate-society articles is in Corporate Governance (+26 a year), Climate / Sustainability (+16), Government Intervention / Regulation (+10) and Corporate Purpose (+8).

What is corporate-society scholarship?

Corporate-society articles are those that examine the corporation as an institution embedded in social, political, environmental, and legal systems — particularly where the focus goes beyond firm value maximization or internal governance mechanics. This includes scholarship on corporate social responsibility, environmental and climate obligations, stakeholder welfare, DEI, corporate purpose debates, and social enterprise. Articles whose object remains internal to the firm — traditional corporate law doctrine, governance mechanics, or financial regulation — are classified as firm-focused.

Corporate-Society Tone Analysis: Affirming vs. Skeptical

Among the 1,123 corporate-society articles, each was classified by its normative treatment of the corporate-society issue. An affirming classification entails a normative argument in favor of corporate action to advance social, environmental, political, or ethical objectives. A skeptical classification entails a critical or skeptical view of the corporation’s role in achieving social or environmental goals; these articles generally take the position that corporations should prioritize shareholder primacy or limit obligations to stakeholders. One article that takes no position is coded Unresolved and is excluded from the charts.

Methodology

Stage 1 — Data gathering and cleaning

I worked with law librarians to develop and refine Boolean searches in LexisNexis and Westlaw designed to identify corporate law and corporate governance articles. The initial searches generated more than 7,000 results across the two databases. A research assistant manually downloaded those results. We then cleaned the dataset by removing duplicate articles, articles published in non-U.S. journals, articles published in professional or practitioner-oriented journals rather than scholarly law journals, articles outside the scope of corporate law or corporate governance, and student-written notes and comments. After these exclusions, the final corpus contained 2,618 unique articles.

Stage 2 — Manual coding

My research assistant and I manually coded a random subset of 874 articles. We first classified each article into one or two of thirty-four predefined corporate law and governance topic areas. We then coded each article for whether its substantive focus fell within the corporate-society construct. In the coding scheme and the downloadable data, this corporate-society classification carries the label BLS (business, law, and society); the two terms refer to the same category. Articles were coded as "0" if they did not fit the definition and "1" if they did. For articles coded corporate-society, we also coded the author's treatment of the issue — specifically, whether the article was supportive of or critical of a corporate-society approach.

Stage 3 — AI-assisted coding

We used Harvey AI to apply the coding protocol to the full corpus. Harvey allowed us to apply a uniform coding workflow to a large set of documents. We uploaded the articles and ran a workflow that generated structured responses for each document, including the article's corporate law topic, corporate-society classification, treatment of the corporate-society issue, author, year, and journal. Harvey returned the results in tabular form, which allowed us to sort, filter, spot-check, and export the data for analysis.

To validate the AI-assisted coding, we compared Harvey's classifications against the manually coded subset. The manual coding served as a benchmark for evaluating whether Harvey applied the coding protocol consistently. We then revised the prompts and coding instructions to improve consistency between the manual and AI-assisted coding. We also validated Harvey's codes using Claude Model 5.1, which we also manually validated. The final analysis therefore combines close manual review with AI-assisted coding at scale.

Limitations

All corporate-society and topic classifications are based on the researcher's judgment. The dual-tag limit means articles spanning three or more themes may be simplified. Boolean search terms may not capture all relevant scholarship, and coverage depends on the completeness of Westlaw and LexisNexis databases. Two topic categories are reported under updated labels: articles classified "Antitrust" are reported as "Competition & Market Power," and articles classified "Bankruptcy" are reported as "Corporate Bankruptcy & Restructuring." The classification definitions are unchanged.
